## What I would prioritize for yours

My first product demonstration would not be “look, it has 100 menu entries.”

It would be:

“Wire this machine using the generated diagram. Identify the switches. Square the gantry. Probe the stock. Check the clamps in the preview. Run the job. Interrupt the interface. Reconnect without losing state. Then deliberately create a fault and have the controller explain what happened.”

That combines the setup appeal of Acorn, the configurability of Mesa, and the practical usability that keeps people attached to their existing printer or laser ecosystem.

I would put guided commissioning, probing, preflight, restart, diagnostics, and complete backups into the core product. Sensor-heavy inspection, advanced printing, and specialized process interfaces can be supported through properly defined modules.

One important architectural decision follows: printer features need a validated execution path of their own. I would not assume Klipper toolheads or pressure-advance behavior become compatible merely because LinuxCNC runs on the same computer. Whether functions are ported or supported through an explicitly selected backend, only one system should own motion at a time. Klipper’s documented host/microcontroller architecture illustrates why that integration is substantive work. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"}

## 1. First: different kinds of threading need different connections

| Operation | What the controller actually needs |
|---|---|
| Lathe single-point threading | Axis control plus spindle-angle feedback and a repeatable spindle index. LinuxCNC provides G33 and G76 for synchronized threading. |

## 4. Lathe threading: encoder counts and thread counts are different

The encoder tells the controller how the spindle is moving. The programmed thread lead tells it how far the cutting axis should travel for each spindle revolution.

LinuxCNC’s spindle interface uses measured revolutions and velocity, plus index handling, for synchronized motion. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

For our controller, I would require full A/B/index feedback for the advertised threading-and-rigid-tapping configuration:

- A and B provide angular movement and direction.
- Index establishes a repeatable rotational reference.
- Hardware counters preserve those events independently of the screen refresh.

For a single-start thread:

```text
Pitch in mm = 25.4 ÷ threads per inch
Axis feed in mm/min = pitch × spindle RPM
```

Example:

```text
20 TPI = 1.27 mm pitch

At 600 RPM:
1.27 × 600 = 762 mm/min axial feed
```

For a multi-start thread, the synchronized travel per revolution is the lead:

```text
Lead = pitch × number of starts
```

So a two-start, 2 mm-pitch thread requires 4 mm of axial travel per spindle revolution.

None of those values require an encoder with a matching number of pulses. A 1,024-cycle/revolution quadrature encoder normally produces 4,096 counts/revolution with ×4 decoding. Manufacturer terminology must be checked because “PPR” and “CPR” are not always used consistently. HostMot2 documents the ×4 relationship. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

I would include a commissioning test that measures counts over a known number of actual spindle revolutions and verifies direction and index.

For a geared encoder arrangement, my preferred standard would be either a 1:1 spindle encoder or correctly scaled quadrature feedback combined with a spindle-true index. I would not assume the encoder shaft’s index is automatically one pulse per work-spindle revolution.
