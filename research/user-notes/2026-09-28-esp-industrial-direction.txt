## Give the ESP32 a real automation role

I would divide responsibility like this:

| System | Its job |
|---|---|
| LinuxCNC | Machining program, coordinated motion, threading, tapping, and motion-dependent machine sequences |
| FPGA | Precisely timed motor commands, encoder counting, fast event capture, and hardware command timeout |
| ESP32-S3 | Supporting-equipment automation, condition monitoring, diagnostics, and service access |
| Independent protection hardware | Defined fault inhibition and the interfaces to the machine’s appropriate safety system |

That extends your existing Linux/FPGA/ESP architecture without creating a second motion controller. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

Here is what I would actually assign to the ESP32:

| Function | What it would do | What it needs to verify the result |
|---|---|---|
| Cabinet cooling | Control fans and continue approved cooling after machining stops | Temperature sensors and fan-tach feedback |
| Coolant and air services | Execute requests, wait for readiness, and report inadequate conditions | Flow, pressure, or level sensors |
| Lubrication | Run approved timed cycles and track consumption or usage | Pressure, level, or delivery confirmation |
| Electrical health | Record supply problems, overload indications, and abnormal temperatures | Voltage/current monitoring and dedicated power supervisors |
| Accessory automation | Operate work lights, extraction requests, and other permitted auxiliaries | Appropriate output drivers and feedback where required |
| Controller diagnostics | Detect missing health messages, record fault sequences, and provide a separate service interface | Direct wired communication and independent observations |

The key is checking outcomes—not just commands.

For example, I would want the screen to report:

```text
COOLANT FLOW LOST

Pump command: ON
Measured flow: Below configured minimum
Machine response: Controlled hold requested
Restart: Blocked pending verification
```

That does not pretend to know whether the cause is a blocked filter, empty tank, failed pump, or broken sensor. It tells the operator what was observed and what to check.

Most of this needs ordinary state machines, timers, thresholds, and sensors—not AI. AI could explain the recorded evidence later.

### One controller must own each output

I would use a request-and-confirm arrangement:

```text
LinuxCNC: “Prepare coolant.”
ESP32:   Starts the approved sequence.
ESP32:   “Flow confirmed” or “Fault.”
LinuxCNC: Continues or refuses the operation.
```

The ESP32 should not independently fire the torch, release the drawbar, or start a toolchanger because one sensor happened to change. Those actions need the main machine state and their interlocks.

Also, once the ESP32 controls an essential service, its failure matters. We cannot promise “the ESP32 can crash without affecting machining” while depending on it for coolant verification. Its timeout must produce a defined machine response.

## An ESP32 does not prevent industrial-grade construction

For example, Espressif specifies the ESP32-S3-WROOM-1-N8 module for −40°C to +85°C ambient around the module. That is a legitimate component specification—but it does not give the finished controller, touchscreen, power supply, or enclosure the same rating. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

The important engineering is around it.

I would give the helper its own protected regulator branch and direct access to selected sensors. Otherwise, an ESP32 that only repeats information supplied by Linux cannot independently diagnose Linux.

Power design matters here: Espressif explicitly warns that radio transmissions can cause sudden current demand and rail collapse without suitable supply design and decoupling. Its hardware guidance also discusses power-monitor circuitry for unstable supply conditions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

And the ESP32 should not be the only watchdog. An external watchdog circuit provides monitoring outside the processor’s own firmware; TI’s TPS3431 is one example of that component category. The actual timeout, monitored signals, and resulting machine response still need designing. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

That means an ESP32 firmware freeze must not defeat the fault mechanism intended to handle an ESP32 firmware freeze.

## What “industrial grade” should mean for yours

I would turn it into a qualification specification, not a label on the enclosure.

| Area | What I would require before making the claim |
|---|---|
| Power input | A documented operating range; reverse-polarity, transient, overcurrent, and brownout behavior tested |
| Machine I/O | Defined voltage/current ratings, protected field connections, suitable isolation, and no unintended active outputs during boot or reset |
| Electrical noise | Testing for ESD, fast transients, surge where applicable, and conducted/radiated interference on the finished assembly |
| Temperature and contamination | A verified assembled-unit temperature range and an enclosure/cooling arrangement suited to the declared shop environment |
| Software behavior | Bounded queues, validated configurations, explicit timeouts, rollback-capable updates, and no automatic restart after faults |
| Manufacturing | Exact component control, serial numbers, calibration records, and a documented functional test for every shipped unit |
| Serviceability | Replaceable terminals/modules, readable fault history, available recovery images, and published compatibility information |

Established industrial controllers publish these kinds of limits. For example, Beckhoff documents operating temperature, vibration/shock performance, protection rating, and EMC immunity/emissions standards for its CX7031—not merely its processor model. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

Your qualification lab should determine the standards and test levels appropriate to the finished product and intended markets. Testing one bare board beside one VFD would be useful development evidence, but not a complete industrial qualification.

Your machined HDPE housing can remain in the design. I would integrate the necessary internal shielding, bonding, cable termination, and mechanical support rather than assume the plastic enclosure—or a metal backplate alone—solves the electrical-noise problem.

There is one distinction to preserve: industrial reliability and safety-rated control are separate claims. Safety-related control systems require their own design and validation process; ISO 13849-1 addresses those systems. I would retain an appropriate independent E-stop/interlock/drive-safety arrangement rather than present the ESP32 as a safety PLC. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

Also, the fault response cannot simply be “everything off.” We must define which equipment stops, which cooling continues, and how workholding and vertical axes remain supported.
